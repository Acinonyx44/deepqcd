Summary

Two follow-ups to the finance discussion, both built on the original DeepQCD code (the network, its training and the window-limited CUSUM, from original_qcd.py). Code: rare_prior.py, polymarket_fetch.py, polymarket_qcd.py; notes: rare-events.md, polymarket.md.

  • A. Rare changes (credit, money laundering, delinquency). Can DeepQCD be trained where changes are common and used where they are ten times rarer? For detection, yes: the network trained at the paper's rate ρ = 10⁻³ and run at ρ = 10⁻⁴ is within one step of the optimal Shiryaev rule at every false-alarm level. Its output is not the deployment posterior, though. Read as a probability it over-alarms. The theoretical prior correction alone over-corrects 25-fold. Calibrating on the enriched data and then applying the correction gives a valid threshold, about twice as conservative as the exact posterior.
  • B. Polymarket (money before the news). In "X by <date>?" markets, does large-trade flow show an event before the price does? We took __NMK__ closed markets with at least $100,000 volume: hourly prices and every trade of $1,000 or more, about 3.1 million market-hours. Only __NEV__ distinct events were surprises with a week of history before them. On the __NTE__ held-out events, at PFA 0.05, DeepQCD alarms in the 24 hours before the public move for 40% of them (20–53% over three seeds). A rule on the 24-hour price rise catches 43%. Buying YES at DeepQCD's alarms earns less than buying at random hours. Trading picks up before events, but its direction follows the price rather than leading it.
A · Rare changes

1Training rate and deployment rate

In credit default, money laundering or delinquency, the change (an account going bad) is rare, perhaps one in ten thousand account-days. Labelled histories are easiest to obtain from enriched samples, in which bad accounts are over-represented. DeepQCD learns the Bayesian posterior P(τ ≤ t | x₁ … xt), and that posterior depends on the change rate ρ. For small ρ, the posterior odds are close to ρ times the Shiryaev–Roberts statistic, so moving from the training rate ρtr to the deployment rate ρdep shifts the log-odds by a constant:

logit pdep = logit ptr + log(ρdep / ρtr).

The shift is monotone, so training at the wrong rate should not change which stopping rules are available: the PFA–ADD curve stays the same. But the rule "stop when the posterior reaches 1 − α", which promises PFA ≤ α, works only if the posterior is right for the deployment rate. For the exact posterior the shift is exact. Shiryaev(10⁻³) shifted by log 0.1 stops with PFA 0.059 / 0.029 / 0.0057 at α = 0.1 / 0.05 / 0.01, and Shiryaev(10⁻⁴) with 0.058 / 0.028 / 0.0057.

Experiment. The paper's §5.1 with p = 1, f₀ = N(0, 1) and f₁ = N(1, 1). Network and training are exactly those of the notebooks: 3,200 streams of 2,000 steps, 20 epochs. Network A is trained with τ ~ geo(10⁻³), the paper's rate, so 85% of its streams contain a change. Network B is trained with τ ~ geo(10⁻⁴) on the same budget; 18% of its streams contain a change. The deployment test uses τ ~ geo(10⁻⁴), truncated at 60,000 steps, over 3,000 streams. The references are the optimal Shiryaev(10⁻⁴) and the misspecified Shiryaev(10⁻³). "Calibrated" means a logistic fit, logit P(τ ≤ t) = a · logit dt + b, on 500 labelled streams at the training rate. It uses enriched data only (a = __PA__, b = __PB__).

  • Detection does not depend on the training rate. So the network can be trained where labelled changes exist.
  • The raw output over-alarms. Read as a probability, A's output gives PFA 0.137 at α = 0.1.
  • The prior shift alone over-corrects. It gives PFA 0.004 at α = 0.1 and ADD 24 where 17.5 is possible. The shift is exact only for an exact posterior, and the network is not calibrated even at its own training rate: its log-odds are too low by about 0.9.
  • Calibrate on the enriched data, then shift. Calibrated, A behaves like the exact posterior at its training rate (PFA 0.385 against 0.384 for Shiryaev(10⁻³) at α = 0.1). Shifted, it gives PFA 0.028 / 0.010 / 0.002, all below α. Neither step needs labelled data at the rare rate. It stays conservative because a two-parameter fit cannot follow the network's curve above about 0.8.
  • Training at the true rate does not help. Network B, which saw few changes, is as under-confident as "shifted only".
  • For a bank. Train on enriched data. If held-out data at the true rate exists, set the threshold for the target false-alarm rate on it. Otherwise calibrate on enriched data and shift, and the rule will err on the safe side. ρdep has to be estimated: an error by a factor c moves the threshold by log c.
B · Polymarket

2Why a deadline market is a transient change

A deadline market asks whether something happens before a date, for example "US strikes Iran by March 31?". Its YES price is the market's probability. If the event happens, the market resolves YES at once and the price jumps to about 1. If the date passes, it resolves NO. Suppose some traders learn of the event before the public does. Their buying would push the flow toward YES in the hours before the jump: a change that starts at an unknown time and ends at the public move. A market that resolves NO never has this change. So every market is a stream with or without a transient change, and the paper's transient protocol applies.

3Data

We used Polymarket's public APIs, with no key: the market list (gamma API), hourly YES prices (CLOB prices-history) and every taker trade of at least $1,000 with its wallet (data API trades), as described in the API documentation. We kept closed binary markets whose question has a deadline ("by" or "before" a date; ranges such as "between" excluded) and at least $100,000 volume, from February 2023 to October 2026. Ladder markets that went public within 12 hours of each other count as one event, for example "US strikes Iran by February 28 / March 1 / March 15". Each event enters the test once, through its largest market. Training uses all of an event's markets, plus seven copies of each stream ending 3, 6, …, 21 hours before τpub. The split is by time: markets that closed before __SPLIT__ train the detectors, the rest test them.

The eight hourly features. Flows are signed toward YES: buying YES or selling NO counts as positive.

4Detection

  • DeepQCD: the paper's network and training (LSTM(16) → Dense(10) → Dense(1), Adam, cross-entropy, batch 32, 20 epochs), with label 1 in the change window. It is trained on all eight features, and separately without the two price features ("flow only"), with three seeds each. Features are standardized on no-change training hours, as in the IoT script.
  • Window-limited CUSUM: window_dec_stat over the last K = 24 hours, on the Gaussian log-likelihood direction of the change window against the rest of the training data. Again with all features, and flow only.
  • Price rules: the YES price itself, and its rise over the last 24 hours.

5Does the money move before the price?

Large trades per hour, in the held-out data. "Net toward YES" is net large-trade money toward YES divided by gross large-trade money (−1: all toward NO, +1: all toward YES).

Event markets are busier than others even a week before the event, because they are the markets in the news. Activity jumps again in the last 24 hours, to about ten times its level earlier that week. The net direction of large trades moves from leaning toward NO to balanced, but the price rises at the same time (from 0.16 to 0.25 on average). Wallets that are new to a market, or new to Polymarket, do not lean toward YES before events. In hourly aggregates the flow moves with the price, not ahead of it.

The test that matters for a trader: buy YES at a detector's first alarm, provided the price is still below 0.8, and hold to resolution, paying 1 cent. Each held-out market trades at most once, with each detector's threshold from Table __TDET__. If the alarms knew something the price did not, they would beat buying at a random hour.

threshold at PFA

No detector beats random entry. In this period YES was cheap on average (+5.7 cents per contract at random hours), because many of the war-related events in 2026 did happen. Alarms from the price rise lose heavily: most price run-ups are scares that fade. DeepQCD with all features inherits part of that. Flow-only alarms are about break-even. Nothing in the hourly large-trade data is worth more than the price already says.

6Verdict and next steps

  • Feasibility: weak. At hourly resolution with trades of $1,000 or more, there is no sign of systematic informed flow before public events that a detector could exploit. The price is already the best summary.
  • Too few events. There are __NEV__ surprise events in three and a half years, concentrated in 2026 and in a few themes (Iran, Venezuela, Russia–Ukraine), with 34 left for training. The three DeepQCD seeds give PD 0.47, 0.20 and 0.53 at PFA 0.05 (flow only: 0.67, 0.17, 0.07), so the network is not stable at this sample size.
  • What could still work. Minute-level data with every trade, around the publicly reported cases of suspected insider bets. Wallet-level features, such as a wallet's past accuracy, instead of hourly totals. Pooling with Kalshi. Or surveillance instead of trading: the activity surge before events is real, and an exchange could flag markets for review with it.
  • Use. Trading on material non-public information is illegal in many markets. The aim here is detection, for market surveillance.
